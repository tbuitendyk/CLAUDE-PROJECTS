The edge pays after fees — and 86% of it goes to the fees

tl;dr the money test came back positive and it beat all 19 scrambled worlds on
every single measure, which is the first unambiguous result this project has
produced. Then I audited it, and the important number was one that appeared in
no table until I went looking: fees eat 86% of the gross edge, leaving 16%
headroom over what we ASSUME trading costs. No live money follows from this.
The next test — does it survive in a different period at all — is running now,
because every scramble so far came from the same four-month window.

THE RESULT

TABLE 1 -- REAL MARKET DATA AGAINST 19 SCRAMBLED WORLDS, ON MONEY
What it measures: dollars on data no search ever touched, after fees. The 19
scrambled worlds have nothing to predict, so they show what luck pays. Beating
all 19 is the strongest claim 19 draws allow.

  measure              real     luckiest scramble    beats
  net $            1,469.95              380.45      19/19
  median setup $       9.88                3.59      19/19
  setups in profit    55.9%               52.4%      19/19
  $ per trade        0.0412              0.0091      19/19

  KEY  real              = live market data, held-back slice
       luckiest scramble = the best of 19 worlds with nothing to predict

       vs buy-and-hold   = net dollars minus what simply holding would have made

Sanity check passed: 18 of the 19 scrambled worlds LOSE money, as anything
paying fees with nothing to predict must. Margin over the luckiest scramble:
$1,089.50 — a real margin, unlike cycle 6's 0.24 of a point.

It also beat simply buying and holding by $1,386, so this is not just long
exposure in a rising market.

NOW THE PART THAT MATTERS MORE

TABLE 2 -- WHERE THE MONEY ACTUALLY GOES, PER TRADE
What it measures: the same result broken into what the strategy earns before
costs, what costs take, and what is left. Each trade is $100 of stock.

  gross earned before costs    $0.2912   (0.291% of the $100 traded)
  trading costs assumed        $0.2500   (0.250%)
  left over                    $0.0412   (0.041%)

  KEY  gross  = what the strategy makes before any fees
       costs  = Binance fees plus a spread allowance, as ASSUMED by the
                simulator, not measured on the venue
       (all DOLLARS per trade on $100 traded)

Fees take 86 cents of every dollar the strategy earns. Break-even is at 0.291%
per round trip and we are assuming 0.250%. That is 16% of headroom.

Binance's own taker fee is 0.10% per side, so 0.20% round trip before any
spread or any slippage. If real execution costs 0.30% — which an ordinary bad
fill or a worse fee tier would do — THE EDGE IS NEGATIVE.

This did not appear anywhere until step 7 made me ask what the net number was
hiding. It is now a permanent reporting rule: gross, fees and break-even go
beside every net figure.

THREE THINGS THE HEADLINE MUST NOT BE READ AS

1. $1,469.95 IS NOT PROFIT WE WOULD HAVE MADE. It is the sum over 170 setups
   across 17 coins — about ten overlapping configurations per coin, all held
   at once. Nobody could trade that. It is a statistical aggregate whose only
   job is being compared against the same aggregate computed on noise.

2. THIS DOES NOT CONFIRM THE ACCURACY RESULT. The money figure comes from a
   setup chosen by money; the accuracy figure from a rung chosen by accuracy.
   35,678 trades against 19,913 calls — different selections, different
   objects. Neither validates the other. I nearly wrote that it did.

3. IT RESTS ON ONE FOUR-MONTH WINDOW. Every one of the 19 scrambles was drawn
   from the same held-back slice. A scrambled world tests "better than noise
   in this period"; it cannot test "holds in another period", because the
   noise shares the period.

WHAT IS RUNNING

  bracketlab-20260729-1328-l10-walkforward-2025-06     ~5 hours

The identical test with the data ending June 2025, so the held-back slice
lands on a completely different stretch of history with no overlap. Rule fixed
before firing: beats all 19 and the edge is not a property of one period;
weaker and cycle 9 was period-specific, which is the answer rather than an
invitation to try windows until one works.

My expectation, on the record ahead of the numbers: I expect the DIRECTION to
survive, because the margin was wide. What I doubt is the SIZE, since 86% of
it is already going to fees and a different period could push net through
zero.

CODE UPDATE (standing rule)

A multi-scramble job now carries its own real arm. Cycle 8 spent five hours
measuring 19 scrambles with nothing to compare them against, because the loop
that generates them started at scramble 1 and never produced the unscrambled
case; the real arm came from a different job on an older build. I had written
that up as a discipline rule — "re-run both arms after a census change" — and
then the very next run needed it. Discipline was the wrong fix. Both arms now
ride in one job by construction: one build, one data range, one moment.
Reverting it fails the test suite.

FOUR NEW QC ITEMS (34-37), all from auditing my own run

  34  A census change does not apply to runs already made.
  35  Two numbers from one run need not describe the same configuration.
  36  A census total is not achievable profit.
  37  A positive net result is not robust. Report gross, fees and break-even
      beside every net figure — 86% eaten is invisible in the net alone.

WHERE THIS LEAVES US

The system predicts direction better than noise, and after fees it makes money
against noise, in one window, on a measure that cannot be traded directly. The
honest position: this is the first thing here worth continuing to test, and it
is a long way from being worth funding. The order is walk-forward first, then
execution cost measured on the actual venue rather than assumed in a
simulator, and only then any conversation about stake.
