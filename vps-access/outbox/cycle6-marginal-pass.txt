Cycle 6: a pass, but the weakest one the design can produce

tl;dr the system beat all 19 scrambles on the measure that matters, which is
the first time anything here has cleared an honest test. Do not celebrate yet.
It cleared by 0.24 of a point, the "0.05" is the best number 19 scrambles can
ever produce rather than a strong result, and two of the three measures say it
FAILED. On top of that the real half of the comparison was produced by older
code and has never been re-checked. I have not gone on to the money question.
Instead I fired a 22-minute run to re-measure the real half on today's build,
because a 0.24-point margin cannot absorb any drift at all. Your three
interface requests are built, tested and deployed.


THE RESULT, READ THROUGH THE RULE I FIXED IN ADVANCE

Gate first, as declared: the 19 scrambles had to agree within 2.5 points.
They spanned 2.44. Passed -- with 0.06 of a point to spare, which is close
enough to having binned the whole run that it is worth saying out loud.

TABLE 1 -- DOES IT PREDICT DIRECTION BETTER THAN NOISE?
What it measures: of the moments where the system actually committed to a
trade, how often it was right. Scrambled runs have nothing to predict, so
they show what luck alone earns. Higher is better; only the gap matters.

  arm                          score     spread
  real market data            36.09%     single measurement
  19 scrambled runs           34.48%     33.41% - 35.85%
  gap (the skill part)        +1.61 pts

  KEY  score   = share of committed up/down calls that were correct
       spread  = lowest to highest across the 19 scrambled runs
       (accuracy percentages, NOT money)

Verdict per the declared rule: above all 19 -> p = 0.05.

THREE REASONS TO HOLD THE CHAMPAGNE

1. 0.05 IS THE FLOOR, NOT THE FINDING. With 19 scrambles the strongest claim
   arithmetic permits is 1 in 20. So "p = 0.05" here means "the best this
   design could possibly have said", not "a strong effect".

2. THE MARGIN IS 0.24 OF A POINT. One scramble reached 35.85%. We reached
   36.09%. That is the entire result.

3. TWO OF THREE MEASURES SAY IT FAILED.

TABLE 2 -- THE SAME RUN, THREE WAYS OF SCORING IT
What it measures: whether the conclusion depends on which yardstick is used.
It does, and that disagreement is itself a finding.

  measure                  real     scramble range    verdict
  directional accuracy    36.09%    33.41 - 35.85%    ABOVE all 19
  active-only share       56.6%     48.6  - 58.8%     INSIDE, 2.2 below top
  all-units share         57.6%     51.8  - 59.4%     INSIDE

  KEY  directional accuracy = of committed trades, share correct [PRIMARY]
       active-only share    = of setups that traded, share beating their own
                              do-nothing baseline
       all-units share      = same, counting setups that never traded

My declared rule says directional accuracy governs and any disagreement gets
reported loudly. I am honouring that, and I picked it before seeing this. But
a result that passes on my chosen measure and fails on the other two deserves
suspicion rather than a victory lap.

The likely explanation, offered as a story rather than a fact: the headcount
measures squash roughly 22,000 individual decisions down into 170 verdicts,
so their own noise spans 10 points and they simply cannot resolve a 1.6-point
effect. The directional measure keeps the decisions and its noise spans 2.4
points. That favours the primary measure -- but it is reasoning, not proof,
and it is recorded as such.

ONE CHECK THAT CAME OUT CLEAN

I was worried the result might be an artifact of the scrambled runs trading
less often than the real one. It is not:

  pooled across all decisions   real 36.09%   noise 34.48%   +1.61
  typical individual setup      real 35.21%   noise 33.33%   +1.88

Two different ways of averaging, same direction, same size. That objection is
answered.

So the intervening step is running now, ~22 minutes:

  bracketlab-20260729-0729-l7-verify-real-arm

Rule fixed before firing: within 0.1 points of 36.09% and cycle 6 stands, and

YOUR THREE INTERFACE REQUESTS -- BUILT, TESTED, DEPLOYED

1. Every setting that shapes a result now shows on the page for the running
   job, in a table, with the ones that decide what a number MEANS first --
   whether a slice was held back, whether outcomes were scrambled and how,
   whether the census was kept off the money-ranked board. Those were
   invisible before and had to be taken on trust from whatever fired the job.

2. A description field, written when the job is fired and stored with it,
   shown directly beneath the Progress & performance heading. A run with no
   description says so in a warning colour rather than showing blank.

3. Job names now carry their settings. The job above named itself
   "l7-verify-real-arm"; cycle 6 would have been "null19-win-census". The tag
   is INFERRED from the settings rather than typed, so it cannot drift out of
   step with what the job actually did, and holdout=off is called out in the
   name because a run with nothing held back cannot answer an out-of-sample
   question. Names stay safe as filenames and in scripts.

Worth reporting one bug I caused and caught: my first test checked the naming
function in isolation, and a deliberate break showed the job id could stop
using it entirely with the tests still green. A working function that nothing
calls is the same failure as the rest of this week. Fixed, four deliberate
breaks now fail the suite.

THREE NEW QC ITEMS (26-28)

  26  A gate that passes by 0.06 points is not a pass like any other. Report
      the margin, not just the verdict.
  27  The significance floor is not the significance. 19 draws makes 0.05 the
      best possible answer.
  28  Metrics that measure related things need not agree. When they disagree
      that IS the finding, and which one governs must be declared in advance.
