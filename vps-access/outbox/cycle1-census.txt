Cycle 1 census — one real finding, one number I refuse to quote, and cycle 2 firing

tl;dr — Full census, 170 of 170 units, no money filter. Headline is 98/170 holdout-positive at "p=0.027", and I am NOT treating that as evidence, because the 50% null behind it is an assumption I have no right to make. The genuine finding is structural and uncomfortable: search-window edge is ANTI-correlated with holdout edge across chunk shapes, and weekly-8d — the geometry both live paper books run on — has by far the worst collapse of any shape tested. Cycle 2 is running now: the identical census on rotated outcomes, to find out what this statistic does when there is provably nothing to find.

WHY I WILL NOT QUOTE THE p-VALUE

Two reasons, the second more serious than the first.

The units are not independent. Seventeen assets appear in ten branches each,
and crypto assets move together anyway. The effective sample is far below 170,
so any binomial computed on it overstates itself.

But the real problem: the 50% null is ASSUMED. "Edge above zero should happen
half the time by chance" sounds obvious and is not established. Edge here is
accuracy minus the training-majority baseline. A committee whose calls simply
lean toward the modal class scores positive edge with no skill at all;
stand-asides could push it the other way. Nobody knows which without measuring
it — and measuring nulls instead of reasoning about them is the one discipline
this project has held to everywhere else. The edge statistic was quietly
getting a reasoned null. That is exactly the shape of every mistake I have
made today.

THE FINDING THAT DOES SURVIVE

Look at the two rightmost columns as a pair — how much edge is lost between
the window the rung was chosen on and the window nothing touched:

    daily-3d argmax    3.62 -> 3.34    lost 0.3
    daily-4d argmax    3.05 -> 2.72    lost 0.3
    daily-1d dir       5.62 -> 4.53    lost 1.1
    daily-2d argmax    5.86 -> 4.76    lost 1.1
    daily-1d argmax    6.07 -> 3.66    lost 2.4
    daily-2d dir       6.31 -> 3.68    lost 2.6
    daily-4d dir       3.49 -> 0.69    lost 2.8
    weekly-8d argmax  10.77 -> 0.00    lost 10.8
    weekly-8d dir      9.23 -> -3.92   lost 13.2

The geometry with the LARGEST in-sample edge has the WORST out-of-sample
result. That is the textbook signature of overfitting, and it tracks sample
count exactly: an 8-day chunk yields roughly an eighth as many samples as a
1-day chunk, so weekly-8d has the most room to fit noise and duly does.

The uncomfortable part: BOOKS 1 AND 2 RUN ON WEEKLY-8D. This screen says that
is the most overfit-prone shape in the set. It does not invalidate them —
they are forward tests, which is precisely the defence against this, and their
whole point is that they cannot be overfit after the fact. But if you had
asked me which geometry to start a new book on, this says weekly-8d would have
been the worst available choice, and I would not have known that yesterday.

Note also that the two shapes losing least, daily-3d and daily-4d, are the
ones with the smallest in-sample edge. Chasing the biggest search number is
actively the wrong instinct here.

CYCLE 2 — FIRING NOW (bracketlab-20260728-2236)

Identical census, outcomes rotated against features. Everything downstream
survives — autocorrelation, class balance, band calibration, the whole
pipeline — but any real feature-to-outcome relationship is destroyed.

Reading rule, fixed before the result exists:
  * rotated near 85/170 -> the statistic is honest, and cycle 1's 98 is worth
    pursuing, still owed a correction for correlated units.
  * rotated near 98/170 -> the statistic is biased, cycle 1 measured nothing,
    and every edge number so far goes in the bin.
  * rotated well above 98 -> worse again: real data underperforming noise
    would say the features are actively misleading.
